Methodology

How Risk Levels Are Assigned

Last updated: October 28, 2026

Every state and the District of Columbia is assigned one of six risk levels based on documented actions, not predictions, partisanship, or speculation. Each level is built from threat vectors: specific actions, by federal, state, or local officials, that have already happened and are documented by a primary source or original reporting. Risk levels reflect what has already happened, not what might happen.

Threat Vectors

Federal threat vectors

  1. A Justice Department lawsuit for the state's voter data, in any wave and whatever its status.
  2. A federal demand or threat aimed at state or local election officials.
  3. Federal monitors or agents at polling places or election offices.
  4. A federal criminal investigation of the state's elections.
  5. Federal access to voter files or election equipment, obtained or attempted.
  6. A Justice Department lawsuit to block ballots from being counted.

State and local threat vectors. These count when they carry out or coordinate with federal pressure, or restrict voting access, during the 2026 election period.

  1. Running voter rolls through the federal SAVE database, or requiring documentary proof of citizenship.
  2. Cutting early voting days, polling sites, or absentee options, or changing absentee rules after ballots have gone out.
  3. A state or local election body formally asking federal agencies to intervene.
  4. A map drawn after Louisiana v. Callais that eliminates a majority-minority or minority opportunity district.
  5. Officials seizing or destroying election materials outside the lawful process.

How vectors count. Each type of vector counts once per state. A vector stops counting while a court order blocks it for the 2026 election. Refusing a federal demand never lowers a state's level; it earns a "Refused Federal Demand" tag. Battleground status is shown as a tag and does not count toward any level. A vector counts for a state only when the action is aimed at that state; letters or demands sent in the same form to every state are described on the map but do not count for any one state. A state's own transfer of its voter file to the Justice Department is shown by the Complied level and is not counted as vector 5.

Risk Level Criteria

LevelCriteria
Critical A direct attack on election infrastructure or an active election, by any level of government. Applies to Georgia, where the FBI raided Fulton County's election offices and seized 2020 ballots and voter rolls, and Louisiana, where the governor suspended active U.S. House primaries mid-election.
High A Justice Department voter-data lawsuit plus at least one other vector, or any three vectors, or a post-Callais map that eliminates a majority-minority or minority opportunity district. States: AL, AZ, CA, CO, FL, MA, MI, MN, NH, NJ, NV, NY, PA, TN, UT, VA, WI.
Elevated A Justice Department voter-data lawsuit, or any two vectors. States: CT, DC, DE, HI, ID, IL, KY, MD, ME, NC, NM, OK, OR, RI, TX, VT, WA, WV, WY.
Moderate Any one vector. States: IA, MS, OH.
Complied The state gave its voter file to the Justice Department and has no threat vectors. A state that complied and also has a vector gets the level its vectors earn. Per the Brennan Center tracker, 16 states have provided or committed to provide data; Kansas's reported compliance is disputed by the state. Map tiles shown gray: AK, AR, IN, KS, NE, SC, SD.
No Major Action No threat vector has been documented for this state as of the last update. This does not mean a state is safe, only that no qualifying action has been publicly reported. States: MO, MT, ND.

What This Map Does Not Do

This map does not assess the overall health of state election systems, predict future interference, or evaluate partisan gerrymandering. It counts state and local actions only when they carry out or coordinate with federal pressure or restrict voting access during the 2026 election period, and it counts redistricting only when a map drawn after Louisiana v. Callais eliminates a majority-minority or minority opportunity district.

Official and Race Data

Secretary of State and Attorney General names and party affiliations are sourced from the National Association of Secretaries of State (NASS) official roster, cross-referenced with Teri Tome's tracker (updated January 24, 2026) and Ballotpedia for 2026 ballot status. Competitive House race ratings use the Cook Political Report ratings as of their January 15, 2026 update.
